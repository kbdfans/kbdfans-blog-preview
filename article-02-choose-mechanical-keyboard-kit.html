Short answer: Start with layout, then choose the PCB and switch system, confirm case and plate compatibility, and finally check exactly what the kit includes. Do not choose by appearance alone: two components labelled with the same keyboard percentage may still use different mounting points, connectors or layouts.

1. Choose the layout around the keys you use

A compact keyboard is useful only if its shortcuts fit your routine. A 60% layout maximizes desk space but commonly places arrows and the function row on layers. A 65% layout usually adds dedicated arrows. A 75% layout retains a function row, while TKL keeps a more separated navigation cluster.

Also check ANSI versus ISO, bottom-row sizes, split options and blocker style. Percentage is a category, not a complete compatibility specification.

2. Decide between MX mechanical and Hall Effect

Conventional MX-style mechanical switches use electrical contacts. Hall Effect keyboards detect magnetic switch movement and may support features such as adjustable actuation when the PCB and firmware provide them. Their switches and PCBs are not interchangeable simply because the cases share a size. Choose the system first, then verify every component against that system.

Choose MX mechanical when broad switch choice and conventional mechanical compatibility are the priority. Consider Hall Effect when the specific PCB, firmware and magnetic switches provide the input behavior you need. In either case, verify the exact switch list: “Hall Effect” is a technology category, not a promise that every magnetic switch works with every HE PCB.

3. Choose hot-swap or solder

Hot-swap sockets let you install compatible switches without soldering and make switch changes easier. They are usually the more approachable choice for a first build.

Solder PCBs require tools and assembly skill, but some offer more layout options. Check the PCB layout diagram before assuming either version supports split Backspace, split Shift, ISO or other alternatives.

Browse current keyboard PCBs, but use the product compatibility statement—not the collection label—as the final authority.

4. Understand mounting style and materials

Mounting style describes how the plate or PCB is supported inside the case. Gasket, top-mount, tray-mount and other systems can feel and sound different, but material, plate cuts, foam, switches, keycaps and assembly also influence the result. Avoid treating one mounting label as a guarantee of a specific sound.

When choosing a case or plate, confirm the exact product family, revision and layout.

Plate material is only one part of the result

Aluminum, polycarbonate, FR4, carbon fiber and other plate materials can change stiffness, feedback and sound, but they work together with plate cuts, mounting style, foam, switches, keycaps and case construction. Select a plate that is explicitly supported by the kit and layout first. Treat sound clips as references rather than guarantees.

Stabilizers and non-standard keys

Larger keys such as Spacebar, Enter, Backspace and Shift use stabilizers. Confirm whether the PCB expects screw-in, clip-in or another stabilizer format, and check the required sizes. A non-standard bottom row, split key or regional layout can also change the stabilizer and keycap requirements.

5. Check what the kit includes

“DIY kit” does not always mean a complete keyboard. Before checkout, identify whether the selected configuration includes:

  • Case
  • PCB and daughterboard, if applicable
  • Plate
  • Mounting hardware and foams
  • Stabilizers
  • Switches
  • Keycaps
  • Cable

Read the selected variant’s contents, not only the page title. Configurable products may change included parts as options change.

6. Check firmware, connectivity and operating-system needs

Firmware determines how layers, shortcuts, lighting and remapping work. Before ordering, confirm what configuration software the selected PCB uses, whether it supports your operating system, and whether the required instructions are available. Do not assume that two PCBs offered for the same case use identical firmware or features.

Connectivity also changes the parts list. A wired PCB needs a compatible cable and connector position. A wireless or multi-mode PCB may require a battery and can have specific installation, shipping or charging requirements. Confirm whether the battery is included, sold separately or unavailable for certain destinations. If wireless operation is essential, verify it on the exact PCB option rather than the overall kit name.

7. Verify the layout at PCB, plate and keycap level

Layout support has three layers. The PCB must electrically support each key position. The plate must have matching cutouts. The keycap set must include the required sizes and legends. A solder PCB may support several alternative layouts while one plate supports only a subset; a hot-swap PCB may lock each socket to one position.

Use the layout diagram to check the bottom row, right Shift, Backspace, Enter and Spacebar. For ISO, split layouts or unusual blockers, verify each component separately. This prevents the common situation where the case and PCB match but the plate or keycaps do not.

Before checkout: take a screenshot or save a note of the final configuration. Configurator choices may change the PCB, plate, case, weight or included accessories, and that saved record makes later compatibility checks easier.

Plan the build before setting a budget

The kit price may not represent the cost of a complete keyboard. Build a parts list first, then compare the total. Include switches, stabilizers, keycaps, cable, tools and any optional foam or weight you genuinely want. This prevents a visually appealing kit from becoming an incomplete build.

Also separate necessary parts from tuning parts. A compatible PCB, plate and stabilizer set are necessary. Extra foam, alternate plates and decorative weights are preferences. Start with the functional build, then tune it after you understand the baseline feel and sound.

How to compare two shortlisted kits

Create a side-by-side list with six rows: layout, PCB choices, mounting system, plate choices, included parts and firmware. Add a seventh row for parts you still need to buy. This exposes meaningful differences without relying on marketing language. If both kits satisfy the technical requirements, then compare appearance, material and tuning options.

Give extra weight to documentation and replacement-part availability when buying a first kit. Clear assembly information can save more time than a minor specification difference. If a product page leaves an essential compatibility question unanswered, resolve it before ordering rather than planning to adapt parts later.

What tools does a first build need?

A hot-swap build commonly needs a suitable screwdriver, switch puller and keycap puller. Stabilizer preparation may need small tools and lubricant if you choose to tune them. A solder build additionally requires appropriate soldering equipment, ventilation and safe handling knowledge. Follow the PCB and kit instructions because screw types and assembly sequences vary.

Do not force a switch, connector or case screw. If a part resists, stop and check its orientation and compatibility. Photographing each assembly stage can help if you need to retrace the cable routing or mounting order.

Recommended assembly order for a first build

  1. Inspect the case, PCB, plate and included hardware.
  2. Test the PCB before final assembly using the manufacturer’s recommended method.
  3. Prepare and install compatible stabilizers.
  4. Install the plate and switches according to the selected mounting system.
  5. Test every key before closing the case.
  6. Complete final assembly, then configure firmware and layers.

Final recommendation

Choose the kit that makes your required layout and compatibility easiest to verify. For a first build, a clearly documented hot-swap configuration can reduce assembly complexity, but it is not automatically the right choice for every layout. Confirm the complete system—case, PCB, plate, stabilizers, switches, keycaps and firmware—before comparing optional tuning parts.

Compatibility checklist

  1. Match the exact product name and revision across case, PCB and plate.
  2. Confirm ANSI/ISO and every non-standard key size.
  3. Confirm MX or Hall Effect compatibility.
  4. Confirm hot-swap sockets support the intended layout.
  5. Confirm stabilizer type and sizes.
  6. Check switch and keycap quantities, including spare switches.
  7. Review current sales status and estimated fulfillment wording.
  8. Save the final configuration before ordering accessories.

Frequently asked questions

What else do I need after buying a keyboard kit?

It depends on the selected kit. Many require switches, stabilizers and keycaps; some also require a cable or tools. Treat the product’s included-parts list and selected options as the source of truth.

Is hot-swap always better for beginners?

It is usually easier because it removes soldering from the initial build and allows later switch changes. A solder PCB may be preferable when you specifically need an alternate layout that its hot-swap counterpart does not support.

Will any 65% PCB fit any 65% case?

No. USB placement, mounting points, daughterboards, plate geometry and internal dimensions can differ. Match the exact case and PCB compatibility lists.

Should sound tests decide which kit I buy?

Use them as references, not guarantees. Microphone, room, desk, switches, keycaps, foam and assembly can change the recorded result. Layout and compatibility should come first.
